Dzud

Mongolian has a word for a winter that kills livestock by the million. I went through fifty-five years of national records to learn what makes one, and how early you can see it coming.

Analysis and design by Timur Kherlenbayar
Scroll

A dzud (зуд, said roughly like zood) is a whole winter in which snow, ice or cold seals the pasture so tightly that animals can no longer reach the grass underneath. They weaken for weeks and then die, mostly between February and May, long after the weather that doomed them.

Mongolia's herds graze outdoors all year, and there are no barns full of feed to fall back on. When the grass is out of reach, the herd is the only thing a family has left to lose. Herders even tell the different kinds of dzud apart by colour.

  • WhiteSnow so deep that animals cannot dig down to the grass.
  • IronA thaw that refreezes and locks the pasture under a sheet of ice.
  • BlackBare frozen ground with no grass to eat and no snow to drink.

One winter

Start with a single year. In December 2009, Mongolia's annual livestock census counted 44 million animals: sheep, goats, cattle, horses and camels. Each dot here stands for 50,000 of them.

By the end of 2010, 10.3 million of those animals were dead. That is almost one in every four, gone in a single year.

For a herding family, animals are the savings account, the paycheck and the pantry all at once. In Uvurkhangai, the province hit hardest that year, nearly half of all livestock died.

It had happened before, and it would happen again.

Fifty-five years of counting

Mongolia counts its animals every December and has published the totals back to 1970. Put the census next to each year's death toll and the country's recent history is easy to read.

1970 to 1990

For two decades the national herd hardly moved. Under the socialist collectives it stayed between 22 and 26 million animals, and even a bad winter took less than a tenth of them.

The 1990s

In the early 1990s the collectives were dissolved and the animals passed into private hands. With no quotas to hold them back, families kept as many as they could. By 1999 the herd had reached 33.6 million.

2000 to 2002

Then three hard winters arrived back to back. Mongolia lost 11.2 million animals in three years, and the herd fell to where it had been in the 1970s.

2010

The recovery was quick. Seven years later the herd stood at a record 44 million. Then came 2010, the deadliest single year in the whole record.

2011 to 2022

After 2010 the herd climbed higher than it had ever been, passing 71 million animals. That is about twenty for every person in the country.

Much of the growth was goats, kept for their cashmere. There were 5 million of them in 1990 and 27.6 million in 2022.

2023 and 2024

The last two hard winters took 14.3 million animals between them. In raw numbers, 2024 was nearly as deadly as 2010.

The three worst years in this 55-year record have all come since 2000.

Where the animals died

National totals hide the most important thing about a dzud, which is that it never strikes everywhere at once. Each of these winters had its own geography.

2001
2001

The losses ran through the centre and the north of the country. Govisumber lost 44 percent of its animals, Zavkhan 30 percent and Khuvsgul 26 percent.

2010

This time the west and the Gobi took the blow. Uvurkhangai lost 46 percent and Dundgovi 41 percent. Out on the eastern steppe, Sukhbaatar province lost 3 percent and barely noticed.

2024

Fourteen years later the map flipped. Sukhbaatar, spared in 2010, lost 47 percent of its animals. Uvurkhangai, the worst-hit province of 2010, lost 2 percent.

2024, district by district

Zoom in and it gets starker. Galshar district, in Khentii province, began 2024 with 346,000 animals and lost 89 percent of them. Fourteen districts lost more than half of everything they had.

What makes a dzud

So why Sukhbaatar in 2024 and not in 2010? To find out, I pulled daily records from 41 weather stations along with NASA's rainfall estimates, and described every province's year with three plain questions. Was the summer before it unusually dry? Did snow stay on the ground unusually long? Was the winter unusually cold?

Then I checked each answer against what happened to the animals. I counted a year as a mass die-off when a province lost at least a tenth of its livestock. From 1994 to 2025 that happened 65 times.

672 winters

Each square is one province in one winter. I sorted them into four stacks by how many of the three conditions they met. The red squares are the winters that ended in a mass die-off.

None of the three

When none of the three things happened, a die-off was close to impossible. It occurred 4 times in 196 winters, which is 2 percent.

Cold alone

Cold by itself is not the killer most people assume. Thirty winters were unusually cold but came after a decent summer and had ordinary snow. Not one of them ended in a die-off.

Drought alone, or snow alone

A dry summer on its own, or long snow on its own, raises the odds only a little. Between 3 and 6 percent of those winters went badly.

All three together

Stack all three and everything changes. Of the 74 winters that had a dry summer, long snow and deep cold together, 27 ended in a mass die-off.

That is 36 percent, about eighteen times the risk of a winter with none of them.

The anatomy

A dzud is three ordinary misfortunes lining up. Animals go into winter thin because the summer grass was poor. Snow then buries what little grass there is, and the cold burns through whatever fat they have left.

Try the three switches yourself, and point at any square to see which winter it was.

How early can you see it?

The three conditions do not arrive together. Summer is over by September. Early snow shows up in November and December, and the cold peaks in January. Most of the dying comes later still, from February to May.

That gap is why the question matters. Every week of warning is a week to cut hay, to sell animals while they are still worth something, or to move the herd somewhere better.

Before summer

Before anything is known, the chance that a given province has a mass die-off in the coming year is about 1 in 10.

End of August

By the end of summer the road forks for the first time. After a dry summer the risk is 15 percent. After a normal or wet one it is 5 percent.

New Year

By New Year the two roads are far apart. A dry summer followed by early snow in November and December means a 28 percent risk. If neither happened, the risk is 1.4 percent.

At this point nearly all of the animals that are going to die are still alive.

End of winter

The winter's cold adds the last piece and lifts the worst case to 36 percent. But most of what can be known was already known on the first of January.

One winter in six had both a dry summer and early snow. Those winters produced almost half of all the die-offs.

The heavier tool

I also tried machine learning. I trained a model on 46 weather and herd measurements and tested it the hard way, on years it had never been shown.

Given one winter that ended in a die-off and one that did not, it picked the right one 76 percent of the time. A formula using only the three conditions scored 78 percent. With just 32 winters to learn from, the simple explanation was also the better forecast.

What three numbers can't tell you

I trust the pattern, but I would not want anyone to read it as a law. These are the places where it bends.

Most triple threats still end quietly

A 36 percent risk means that nearly two times out of three, the worst combination did not end in catastrophe. In 2005, eleven provinces met all three conditions and none of them had a die-off. Herders who see a hard winter coming cut hay, sell early and move their animals, and none of that preparation appears in my data.

2024 broke the rule

Sukhbaatar lost 47 percent of its animals in 2024 after a summer that was not dry. The snow that year was simply extreme, and three yes-or-no questions have no way of saying how much.

Eighteen times is a soft number

Die-offs cluster in a handful of years, so I recalculated everything two thousand times with whole years shuffled in and out. The gap stayed large every time, but its plausible range runs from about five times to far more than eighteen.

I expected crowding to matter

The herd has tripled, so I looked for signs that crowded pastures make a hard winter worse. In these records I could not find a clear one. That may be a limit of province-level data and not proof that herd size is harmless.

The three conditions, exactly

  • Dry summer: June to August rainfall more than half a standard deviation below that province's 1982–2000 average.
  • Long snow: days with snow on the ground from November to March, more than half a standard deviation above the 1994–2007 average at nearby stations.
  • Cold winter: December to February temperature more than half a standard deviation below the 1994–2007 average.

Station readings were spread to districts by distance, then averaged to provinces by herd size, so the weather is measured where the animals are. The pattern holds if I move the cut-off: at a quarter of a standard deviation the risk climbs from 1.5 to 29 percent, and at a full one from 2.6 to 67 percent.

What I checked along the way

  • My first version used NASA's modelled snow depth, and it could not see the record snow of 2024 at all. Switching to snow measured on the ground is what made the pattern visible.
  • Every model was scored on whole years it had not seen. A random split would have leaked each winter's weather into its own test and flattered the result.
  • District-level death counts only begin in 2012, so the long analysis uses provinces and the district map starts there.
  • Loss rates divide the year's deaths by the previous December's census. The capital is left out of the analysis.
